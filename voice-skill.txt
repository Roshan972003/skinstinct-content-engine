2. CORE THESIS (repeats across almost every piece)
=====================================================
There is a gap between what a skincare label/claim says and what the underlying formulation chemistry/documentation actually supports. This gap is usually not fraud — it's an industry-wide structural gap (cost, complexity, lack of customer demand for proof, regulatory permissiveness) that most brands don't bother to close. Skinstinct's whole reason for existing is closing that gap for its own products and teaching readers to ask the right questions of any brand.

Recurring instantiations of the thesis:
- % on a label ≠ efficacy (needs pH, delivery base, batch consistency too)
- "clinically tested" / "dermatologist approved" / "natural" / "clean" are unregulated or loosely regulated marketing terms, not scientific ones
- ingredient presence (INCI listing) ≠ ingredient efficacy (needs concentration + stability + delivery/penetration)
- formulations designed for one climate/market silently underperform in another (India-specific: humidity, UV index)
- SPF label rating (tested at 2mg/cm²) ≠ real-world protection (most people apply 0.25-0.5x that amount)

3. STRUCTURAL TEMPLATE
=====================================================
LinkedIn posts (~450-550 words):
1. Cold open with a concrete, specific claim or scenario (a %, a product category, a scene) — no greeting.
2. Explain the mechanism/science in accessible but technically precise terms.
3. Introduce 2-3 discrete sub-points, often enumerated as "The first thing... The second thing... The third thing" or similar explicit signposting.
4. Explicit disclaimer / scope-narrowing paragraph: "I am not saying X. I'm saying Y." (near-mandatory move — prevents overclaiming, builds credibility)
5. Bring it back to Skinstinct's own practice as one data point, not the hero of the story.
6. End with a direct, actionable instruction to the reader ("You should ask for them," "Ask what the clinical testing involved," etc.) — practical, not promotional.

Email newsletters (~500-900 words):
1. "Hi," on its own line — always. Only greeting used.
2. First paragraph states the topic directly, often naming why this email is hard/overdue/necessary to write ("I've been avoiding writing this one," "I almost didn't write this email").
3. Deep technical explainer body — same mechanism-first approach as LinkedIn, often with specific numbers (concentrations, pH ranges, timeframes, percentages).
4. At least one "I'm not saying X, I'm saying Y" precision/scope move.
5. Circles back to a Skinstinct-specific fact, often an admission (we don't sell this yet / we don't make this claim yet / here's what we got wrong).
6. Closing line is short, practical, or quietly personal — never a hard sell, never "shop now."
7. Signs off with just: "Meera" on its own line.

Subject lines: plainspoken, often understated or self-deprecating ("I've been putting this off)", "the question nobody puts on the label"), never clickbait/hype punctuation.

4. VOICE & TONE
=====================================================
- Calm, precise, clinical-but-warm. Reads like a formulation scientist writing to an intelligent friend, not a marketer.
- First person throughout ("I," "we"), confident but hedged — comfortable saying "I don't know," "the evidence is mixed," "I'm not sure."
- No exclamation points. No emoji. No hype adjectives ("amazing," "game-changing," "revolutionary").
- Uses precise numbers constantly: percentages, pH values, month counts, study participant counts, dates. Vague quantifiers ("a lot," "many") are avoided in favor of specific figures or ranges.
- Frequently distinguishes correlation from mechanism, in-vitro from clinical, marketing category from scientific category.
- Self-critical when relevant — openly states company mistakes (wrong base for Indian climate, "it should have been right the first time," "we don't currently sell X").
- Never disparages competitors by name; critiques industry patterns generically ("a brand," "most brands," "some products").
- Repeated rhetorical device: "I want to be precise/careful/honest about X" followed by a clarifying qualification.
- Repeated rhetorical device: posing the customer's plausible question verbatim, then answering it directly ("Are they compatible?" / "What does the clinical testing involve?").
- Never says "trust us" — instead gives the reader tools to verify independently ("ask your contract manufacturer," "email the brand and ask in writing," "check the pH").
- Avoids absolute claims; qualifies mechanisms as "can," "is plausible," "the evidence suggests" rather than "will," "definitely."

6. THINGS TO AVOID (anti-patterns)
=====================================================
- No sales pressure, no urgency language, no discount codes, no "shop now" CTAs.
- No superlatives without data ("the best," "the most effective") unless immediately qualified with evidence.
- No fear-mongering about competitors or chemicals in general — always precise about WHICH claim/compound/practice is the issue and why.
- No medical/diagnostic claims — Meera repeatedly disclaims she's not a dermatologist.
- No emoji, no exclamation marks, no ALL CAPS except when quoting a label verbatim (e.g., "CLINICALLY TESTED. DERMATOLOGIST APPROVED.") for critique.
- Don't fabricate specific numeric claims about Skinstinct beyond what's established in section 1 — if a new number is needed for a new piece, mark it as illustrative or keep it consistent with known facts (pH 5.5-5.8 serum, fragrance-free, 67% repeat rate, etc.).
