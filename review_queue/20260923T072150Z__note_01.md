## Draft (REQUIRES YOUR REVIEW — not published, not sent anywhere)

Most cosmetic batch testing relies on a simple default: sample at the start of the filling run, and sample at the end. If both samples pass specification, the entire batch is signed off and released for packaging.

On paper, that sounds sufficient. In practice, start-and-end sampling leaves a significant blind spot in the middle of a production run.

When a 500-liter or 1,000-liter bulk formulation sits in a holding tank during a continuous multi-hour filling process, several physical variables shift. If mixing speed drops, shear stress changes, or temperature fluctuates across a four-hour run, the emulsion or solution can experience localized stratification.

This creates three subtle quality risks during production:

First, thermal gradients in larger tanks can alter humectant or active distribution during extended filling cycles. The top and bottom layers being drawn off at the beginning and end might hit targets, while the middle portion experiences slight concentration or pH drift.

Second, shear sensitivity varies throughout a run. Continuous pumping through filling heads can alter the micro-structure of a vehicle, quietly changing viscosity or delivery dynamics for bottles filled in the middle of the line without showing up in start or finish testing.

Third, endpoint compliance creates a false sense of consistency. A batch can measure within spec at minute 10 and minute 240, while drifting out of the target window during the middle two hours. Unless a sample is explicitly pulled from the mid-point of the filling run, that drift remains entirely undocumented.

I am not saying contract manufacturers are acting in bad faith, or that products on store shelves are fundamentally unstable. I am saying that standard cosmetic manufacturing protocols are optimized for operational throughput and baseline compliance, not for detecting mid-run variance.

At Skinstinct, mid-batch Certificate of Analysis (CoA) sampling is a non-negotiable requirement in our manufacturing specification for our niacinamide serum. When the CoA for this month's serum run arrived today, the pH held consistently at 5.6 across all three sample points—start, mid-batch, and finish. That pH 5.5–5.8 window is critical for active stability and skin tolerance.

However, actually receiving that mid-batch data point required two follow-up emails with the laboratory. Standard factory workflows default to start and finish checks because intermediate sampling requires pausing or manually pulling vials off an active line. If a brand does not explicitly write mid-batch sampling into their quality agreement and actively follow up to receive it, the test simply does not happen.

The presence of a test certificate is the beginning of the evaluation, not the end of it. If you want to evaluate batch consistency—whether as a consumer reading brand claims or a founder setting up contract manufacturing—do not just ask if a product passed lab testing. Ask how many sampling points were drawn during filling, ask specifically for the mid-batch pH data, and check if the specification held across the entire run, not just at the endpoints.
